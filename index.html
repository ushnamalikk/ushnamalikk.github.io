About

I am a PhD student in Computer Science at the University of Iowa, advised by Rishab Nithyanand. I study AI intermediaries: the chat assistants, AI-generated search answers, and writing assistants that now sit between people and information. My work audits what these systems prioritise, frame, and rewrite before a person ever sees it, and measures what that does to judgment, decision-making, and identity.

Before Iowa I completed a BSc in Computer Science, with Distinction, at the Lahore University of Management Sciences, where I worked with Ihsan Ayyub Qazi, Ayesha Ali, and Zafar Ayyub Qazi on how physicians use large language models and on AI-generated political persuasion. I spent the summer of 2023 at Stanford University.

Research interests: human–AI interaction, AI-mediated search and communication, algorithmic auditing, computational social science.

Research

Current · with Rishab Nithyanand

AI intermediaries in information seeking

Search engines are being replaced by systems that answer instead of retrieve. I am auditing how these AI intermediaries choose and frame their sources. We run controlled, repeated prompt sets across conventional web search and large language models, with and without retrieved documents, on politically contested and health-related queries, and measure whether AI answers narrow or widen the gap between what different groups of people are shown.

Clinical AI · Lahore University of Management Sciences

How physicians use large language models in low-resource settings

In Pakistan, where the physician shortage is acute, I led a mixed-methods study of how 19 practising physicians used ChatGPT-4o while solving six expert-designed diagnostic vignettes, combining every interaction log, graded diagnostic reasoning, and 12 interviews (CHI 2026). As a research assistant I also contributed to two randomised trials of LLM diagnostic assistance, including one (N = 44) testing whether AI-literacy training protects physicians from automation bias when the model's suggestion is wrong (Nature Health 2026; NEJM AI 2026).

AI-mediated communication

Authorship, persuasion, and the assistant in the middle

When an AI assistant rewrites a message, is the author still recognisable? With Moiz Sadiq Awan I introduced the Idiolect Erasure Rate, the drop in authorship-attribution accuracy after AI rewriting, and showed that heavy rewriting removes most of the recoverable authorship signal in personal and workplace writing (CSCW 2026 workshop). Earlier, I led a randomised experiment (N = 197) on whether LLM-generated, microtargeted political videos shift opinions in a Global South setting (in preparation for PNAS Nexus).

Publications

Prompting, Oversight, and Adoption: Physicians' Use of Large Language Models for Diagnostic Reasoning in an LMIC
Ushna Malik*, Laiba Intizar Ahmad*, Amna Hassan, Izzah Shafique, Eilya Mohsin, Ayesha Ali, Muhammad Hamad Alizai, Ihsan Ayyub Qazi
Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), Article 146, 19 pagesOpen access
Mixed-methods study of 19 practising physicians in Pakistan who solved six expert-designed clinical vignettes with optional access to ChatGPT-4o. We analysed every interaction log, had three licensed physicians grade the diagnostic reasoning, and interviewed 12 participants.
  • Physicians used four prompting strategies: authority assertion (role assignment, scaffolding), workflow structuring, clinical reasoning (validation versus reliance), and iterative refinement.
  • Context completeness mattered most. Pasting the full case into the model reached 62.5% diagnostic accuracy, against 39.1% for manually summarised prompts.
  • 42% drifted from cautious scaffolding to direct queries over the session, yet every participant kept verifying the model's output through adversarial prompts and stepwise control.
  • Junior physicians relied on structured scaffolding; seniors cross-checked opportunistically. Interviews framed the model as a "second brain" tempered by concerns about reliability, privacy, and patient trust.

The Assistant Erased You: Measuring Loss of Authorship Signals in AI-Mediated Communication
Ushna Malik, Moiz Sadiq Awan
Workshop on the Broader Impacts of Generative AI in Communication, CSCW 2026Paper talk
Introduces the Idiolect Erasure Rate (IER): the percentage-point drop in authorship-attribution accuracy after an AI assistant rewrites a message. Evaluated on three pre-generative-AI corpora (50 bloggers, 20 Enron email users, 25 Reuters journalists) with a stylometric SVM and the LUAR neural attributer, under light, heavy, and "preserve my voice" rewriting.
  • Heavy rewriting cut LUAR attribution by 66.5 points on personal blogs and 52.5 on workplace email, more than three quarters of the recoverable authorship signal.
  • On beat-structured news the deep attributer barely moved (+1.0, not significant): topic stood in for identity.
  • Even prompts that explicitly instruct the assistant to preserve the author's voice removed most of the signal.
  • Double erasure: heavily rewritten messages also evade AI-text detectors, so they can neither be attributed to their author nor identified as AI-assisted.

AI-Generated Multimodal Political Persuasion Campaigns
Ushna Malik, with Ihsan Ayyub Qazi and Ayesha Ali
In preparation for PNAS Nexus
A randomised controlled experiment (N = 197) in a Global South setting measuring whether LLM-generated, microtargeted political videos, produced with generative text, voice, and video tools, shift participants' opinions. The results speak to the debate over AI-enabled influence operations and what platform safeguards would need to detect.
